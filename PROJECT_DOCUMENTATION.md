## 1. Project Overview

This project aims to identify potentially suspicious patterns in public procurement records and prioritize them for further investigation. Since millions of procurement records are difficult to examine manually, the system uses four evidence modules to analyse different aspects of procurement behaviour.

The system combines the evidence scores while considering their reliability to generate a final risk score. Records are then ranked so that investigators can focus on the highest-priority cases.

The system is designed for post-event forensic screening. A high-risk score indicates that a record deserves further examination; it does not prove that collusion has occurred.

## 2. Problem Statement

Public procurement datasets contain large amounts of information about bids, prices, participating companies, and contract outcomes. Identifying suspicious patterns across this data is challenging because the evidence may be spread across different features, and the availability of information varies between records.

A single indicator may not be sufficient to identify potentially suspicious behaviour. Therefore, the project combines multiple evidence sources and accounts for the reliability of the available information to improve investigation prioritization.

## 3. Objectives

- Analyse large-scale public procurement records.
- Identify suspicious patterns using four complementary evidence modules.
- Estimate the reliability of the evidence generated by each module.
- Normalize module scores using empirical percentile ranks.
- Combine the evidence using reliability-aware fusion and Soft-OR.
- Evaluate the ranking against a baseline using labelled test data.
- Provide an explainable dashboard for investigation prioritization.

## 4. Dataset

The project uses a public procurement dataset containing 3,861,477 records and 22 columns.

| Dataset Category | Number of Records |
|---|---:|
| Total records | 3,861,477 |
| Cartel-labelled records | 8,045 |
| Non-cartel-labelled records | 10,762 |
| Unlabelled records | 3,842,670 |

The dataset includes information such as bid prices, estimated prices, buyers, bidders, tender years, bid counts, winning status, and available cartel labels.

Only 18,807 records have known labels. The remaining records are treated as unlabelled rather than assumed to be non-cartel cases.

## 5. Proposed Methodology

The system follows this workflow:

**Data Input → Preprocessing → Feature Engineering → Four Evidence Modules → Percentile-Rank Normalization → Fusion → Risk Ranking → Explainable Dashboard**

1. **Data input:** Collect public procurement records.
2. **Preprocessing:** Clean data, convert data types, validate prices, and prepare historical and category information.
3. **Feature engineering:** Generate features related to price behaviour, competition, relationships, and statistical patterns.
4. **Evidence extraction:** Run the four modules independently to generate evidence scores and reliability values.
5. **Normalization:** Convert module scores to empirical percentile ranks so that their relative positions can be compared.
6. **Fusion:** Combine the normalized evidence using the baseline reliability-weighted mean or the proposed Soft-OR method.
7. **Risk ranking:** Sort records by their final risk scores and identify priority queues.
8. **Dashboard:** Present rankings and supporting evidence for manual examination.

## 6. Four Evidence Modules

### M1 — Price Behaviour

Examines unusual bid-price deviations relative to estimated prices and comparable procurement groups. It helps identify records with unusual price behaviour.

### M2 — Competition / Participation

Examines participation-related information, including bid counts and single-bid patterns, to assess competition-related signals.

### M3 — Winning / Relationship Behaviour

Examines repeated buyer–bidder relationships, cross-year patterns, and winning concentration to identify potentially suspicious historical relationships.

### M4 — Statistical Forensics

Examines statistical patterns in bid prices, including price dispersion within comparable procurement groups.

Each module produces an evidence score and a corresponding reliability value. The exact calculations are module-specific.

## 7. Reliability and Percentile-Rank Normalization

Evidence strength and evidence reliability are different concepts. Evidence strength represents how unusual a pattern appears, while reliability reflects the dependability of the available information.

The reliability-weighted signal is:

Reliability_j = Availability_j × [0.10 + 0.90 × (ln(1 + n) − L_min) / (L_max − L_min)]

Percentile-rank normalization transforms module scores to a common range from 0 to 1. During evaluation, the reference ranks are fitted using training-period data and then applied to the held-out test period.

This makes the module scores more comparable before fusion. A percentile rank is a relative ranking measure, not a probability of collusion.

## 8. Risk-Score Calculation and Soft-OR Fusion

### 8.1 Baseline: Reliability-Weighted Mean

The baseline method combines the valid module ranks using a reliability-weighted mean:

Baseline Score = sum(r_j × R_j) / sum(R_j)

### 8.2 Proposed Method: Soft-OR Fusion

The proposed Soft-OR method combines the strongest reliability-weighted signal with the weighted mean:

Soft-OR Score = α × max(r_i × q_i)
                + (1 − α) × [sum(r_i × q_i) / sum(q_i)]

The maximum term preserves the strongest available signal, while the weighted mean incorporates evidence from the other valid modules. The final score is used to rank records for investigation; it is not a calibrated probability.

## 9. Evaluation and Baseline Comparison

The project uses a chronological evaluation strategy, with training data from 2004–2015 and held-out test data from 2016–2022.

Five test records had no valid module evidence, leaving 6,203 scored test records, including 225 cartel-labelled records.

| Metric | Baseline Fusion | Soft-OR |
|---|---:|---:|
| ROC-AUC | 0.755930 | 0.758018 |
| Average Precision | 0.093673 | 0.206173 |
| Top-5% precision | 13.50% | 24.12% |
| Cartel-labelled records in Top 5% | 42 | 75 |
| Top-5% recall | 18.67% | 33.33% |

The results show that Soft-OR improves Average Precision and Top-5% precision compared with the baseline. In the highest-ranked 5% of records, Soft-OR identifies 75 cartel-labelled records, compared with 42 using the baseline.

These findings indicate improved prioritization of labelled cases within a limited investigation queue. They do not establish that every high-ranked record involves collusion.

## 10. Limitations

- **Limited labelled data:** Only a small portion of the complete dataset has known labels.
- **Missing and incomplete evidence:** Some records do not contain enough information for every module.
- **Temporal and label bias:** Label availability and the distribution of known cases vary across years.
- **No definitive proof of collusion:** Risk scores indicate suspicious patterns but cannot establish legal wrongdoing.
- **Limited generalizability:** Performance may differ across countries, procurement sectors, buyers, and time periods.

## 11. Conclusion

The project presents a reliability-aware framework for screening public procurement records using four complementary evidence modules. Percentile-rank normalization makes module scores comparable, while Soft-OR combines the strongest available signal with the reliability-weighted mean of the evidence.

On the scored chronological test set, Soft-OR achieves an ROC-AUC of **0.758018** and Average Precision of **0.206173**. Its Top-5% precision is **24.12%**, compared with **13.50%** for the baseline.

The framework can help investigators prioritize records for further examination by presenting ranked results and supporting evidence. Its output should be used as an investigative aid alongside expert review, not as an automatic determination of collusion.
